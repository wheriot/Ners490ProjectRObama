Rittel & Webber (1973) argue that societal planning problems are "wicked"—complex, ill-defined, and lacking objective solutions—unlike "tame" scientific problems. Key properties:

No definitive formulation; problem understanding evolves with solutions.

No stopping rule or clear success criteria.

Solutions are judged good/bad, not true/false.

Unintended consequences make testing solutions impossible.

Every attempt is consequential and irreversible.

Unique contexts prevent standardized solutions.

Problems are symptoms of deeper issues.

Competing explanations reflect stakeholders’ values.
Planners operate in pluralistic societies with conflicting interests, rendering "optimal" solutions illusory. Planning is inherently political, not technical.

Cowan (Journal of Economic History 1990): LWR Lock-in

Pinch & Bijker (SCOT Chapter 1987): Social Construction of Technology (SCOT)
Introduces the Social Construction of Technology (SCOT) framework. Argues technology development is a social process, not linear. Key concepts: Interpretative Flexibility (artifacts have multiple meanings/designs for different groups), Relevant Social Groups (groups defining artifact meaning/problems), Stabilization/Closure (process where flexibility reduces, often via rhetorical means or problem redefinition, not just technical superiority), and Multidirectional Development (rejecting linear innovation models). Advocates symmetric analysis of successful and failed designs.
